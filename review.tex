\section{Background}\label{sec:bg}

\subsection{DRAM Organization}
\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{drambanks_colored.png}
	\caption{High-level DRAM structure}\label{fig:dramstruct}
\end{figure}

Dynamic Random-Access Memory (DRAM) is the most common storage medium used by processors because of its high storage density.
Fig.\ref{fig:dramstruct} illustrates the overall structure of a modern DRAM module. A DRAM module consists of multiple DRAM chips, each featuring several DRAM banks that can be accessed concurrently. Within each bank there are subarrays of densely packed DRAM cells, in which a row of DRAM cells are connected through a \textit{wordline} and a \textit{bitline} joins all cells in a column. Each subarray is isolated from the others since they do not share bitlines. A DRAM cell consists of an access transistor and a capacitor. The bit is stored as electric charge in the capacitor, where the cell represents ``0" when the capacitor is uncharged and ``1" when it is fully charged. When the wordline is activated by putting a voltage on it, all access transistors in the corresponding row are enabled, which allows the charge in the capacitor to be carried onto the bitline. All bitlines eventually connect to a \textit{row buffer} at the edge of the subarray, a DRAM row acting as a cache of the most recently accessed row.

To read from a DRAM row, the system physical address first needs to be translated into physical DRAM address, which specifies banks, rows, columns, etc., and the translation is usually carried out by a programmable \textit{memory controller}\cite{DRAMMapping}. The manufacturer also uses this feature to internally remap bad rows to other locations to tolerate faults. Then, the row is activated, and the row being read is loaded to the row buffer as charge in capacitors storing "1" flows into the row buffer via the bitlines. The sense amplifiers, electric circuits that can detect the small change in voltage on bitlines due to capacitors transferring charge, then amplify the signal to a full logic level (0 or $V_{DD}$, positive power supply voltage). Subsequent read and write operations can be carried out on the row buffer. Due to the destructive nature of this operation (charge flows out), data has to be written back to the capacitors afterwards.

Capacitors inherently leak charge over time. Therefore, the memory controller periodically performs \textit{Refresh} operation to retain data, which will read and rewrite all data in the DRAM so the capacitors are again fully charged. Specifically, memory controller issues a refresh instruction (REF) every $t_{REFI}$, and any row is guaranteed to be refreshed within $t_{REFW}$ (Refresh Window).

\subsection{RowHammer}\label{sec:bg-rowhammer}

RowHammer refers to a DRAM vulnerability such that by changing the voltage in a wordline repeatedly, an attacker can reliably induce bit-flips in nearby rows. They observed that, by frequently toggling wordlines, nearby cells leak their charge at an accelerated rate so that the cells cannot retain their charge before $t_{REFW}$, leading to data loss. A previous study\cite{Walker21} attribute circuit-level disturbance error primarily to two electromagnetic interference phenomena:  \textit{Eletron Injection and Capture}, and \textit{Capacitive Crosstalk}. \cite{Cojocar20} also demonstrated that RowHammer effect exhibits a spatial locality, meaning the number of bit-flips in a row is positively correlated to its distance from the \textit{aggressor row} (\textit{i.e.} the row that is being "hammered"), which leads to the term \textit{blast radius}, the maximum distance an aggressor row can affect.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{rowhammer_thres.png}
	\caption{RowHammer Threshold $T_{RH}$ across different generations of DRAM (Data from \cite{Saxena24,Gloor25}). Overall, $T_{RH}$ continues to decrease, and the rebound in DDR5 is attributed to TRR mechanisms, a RowHammer mitigation, in DDR5 chips\cite{Gloor25}}\label{fig:threshold}
\end{figure}

To launch a RowHammer attack, the aggressor rows must be activated enough times before victim rows are refreshed (\textit{i.e.} within $t_{REFI}$) such that the accumulated disturbance induces bit-flips. As mentioned earlier, refreshing would fully charge all capacitors in a row, which offsets the attacker's effort. Therefore, refreshing victim rows before they experience bit-flips can be used as a defense to RowHammer (further discussed in \ref{sec:refresh-centric}).

Unfortunately, RowHammer vulnerability is worsening over time. As the storage density of DRAM continues to grow, the number of activations required to induce a bit-flip, denoted as $T_{RH}$ (RowHammer Threshold), has been continuously decreasing over the last decade, as illustrated by Fig.\ref{fig:threshold}. As DRAM cell size and cell-to-cell spacing shrinks, they are more susceptible to aforementioned interference. In today's most prevalent DRAM devices, $T_{RH}$ is as low as 10k to 20k. Certain factors, like temperature, aggressor row active time, and physical location of victim cells can further lower this number\cite{Orosa21}. Aggressor rows also have increased blast radius. This trend poses severe risks to new DRAM devices because it is easier to induce bit-flips with fewer activation count, and an aggressor rows can affect more potential victim.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{sides.png}
	\caption{(a) Single-sided RowHammer: only one row is hammered, and the rows within its blast radius are affected. (b) Double-sided RowHammer: two aggressor rows sandwich a victim row in between. (c) Many-sided RowHammer: many rows hammered simultaneously.}\label{fig:sided}
\end{figure}

RowHammer poses a severe security risk to computer systems, as it disrupt \textit{memory isolation}, where accessing a row alters data stored in nearby rows. Many works have demonstrated the far-reaching impact of RowHammer. For instance, \cite{Seaborn15} illustrated that by inducing bit-flips in \textit{Page Table Entry}, a data structure used by the OS to translate virtual memory address to physical memory address, an attacker can gain read and write to arbitrary location in the memory and escalate privilege. Other works have shown that it is capable to leak confidential data, such as Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) weights\cite{Rakin22}, digital signature key\cite{Mus23}, and other processes' memory\cite{Kwong20}. Although no in-the-wild attacks have been reported, a recent survey by Heckel et al.\cite{Heckel26} shows that $12.5\%$ of the tested DRAM are vulnerable to fully automated RowHammer attack, confirming that RowHammer remains a practical and scalable attack vector. 

Over the last decade, numerous mitigations have been proposed and defeated. For instance, some sophisticated variants manipulate the \textit{access pattern}, the sequence, frequency, and timing of memory access, to bypass mitigations. While the naive RowHammer only hammers one row\cite{Kim14}, the double-sided attack hammers two rows across the victim rows at the same time\cite{Seaborn15}, which induces bit-flip in even shorter time. Most recently, many-sided attack hammers many rows simultaneously to crash a widely deployed mitigation\cite{Frigo20}. This will be discussed more in-depth in section \ref{sec:insertion}.

\subsection{Previous Works}

RowHammer has been heavily researched since its first debut, and many mitigation strategies have been proposed accordingly. Many works have attempted to synthesis the knowledge in the field (e.g. \cite{Naseredini23,Loughlin21,Zhang24,Kim25,MutluKim20,Mutlu23,Mutlu23_2}), and some tried to establish a uniform taxonomy for classifying RowHammer mitigations to provide insight for future research. A popular one is based on implementation level, dividing mitigations into either software-based or hardware-based. However, these two categories are too broad, and some recent works (e.g.\cite{Ojha25}) have employed a software-hardware co-design approach, blurring the line between these two categories.

This study instead adopt the taxonomy proposed by Loughlin et al.\cite{Loughlin21}: \textit{isolation-centric, frequency-centric,} and \textit{refresh-centric}. Unlike the conventional taxonomy that classifies defenses according to the implementation level, this framework presents three categories which align with the physical prerequisite for RowHammer: including spatial proximity of aggressor and victim rows, frequent activation of aggressor rows, and victim rows not being refreshed before flipping, respectively. By focusing on functional mechanisms, it sheds light on why many mitigations strategies share similar \textit{failure mode}, the way in which a defense fail, and their performance tradeoffs.

Building on the survey by Naseredini\cite{Naseredini23} which covers the mitigations up to 2023, this work comprehensively classify and evaluate major RowHammer mitigations with an emphasis on the analysis to works published from 2023 onward. This study also breaks down each category of mitigations to analyze their failure modes and tradeoffs, thereby providing insights into building fundamentally robust and efficient mitigations for future studies.

Refresh-centric mitigation approaches aim to refresh potential victim rows, ones within the blast radius of aggressor rows, before they experience bit-flips. Therefore, most refresh-centric mitigations must be implemented on the hardware level, as software lacks the ability to issue refresh to specific physical rows.

Some refresh-centric mitigations are stateful, as they make use of a tracker array. The tracker array stores the activation count of potential aggressor rows. In most cases, due to limited area, the tracker array only keeps track of a subset of active rows. Fig. \ref{fig:refreshpolicies} illustrates an example tracker, where rows are inserted into the tracker when they are highly suspected to be aggressor rows and mitigated when their activation count becomes dangerously high. Rows that do not receive excessive access are evicted to accommodate more aggressive rows.

In this section, I contrast some refresh-centric mitigations based on their insertion policy, eviction policy, and mitigation policy (shown in Table \ref{tab:refreshcomp}) --- an abstraction proposed by Jaleel et al.\cite{Jaleel24} to analyze stateful DRAM trackers. These three policies specify how to determine which rows to add to the tracker array, which rows to remove from the tracker, and which rows to perform mitigation on, respectively. There are three corresponding failure modes, \textit{Tracker Insertion Failure (TIF)}, \textit{Tracker Retention Failure (TRF)}, and \textit{Tardiness}. Finally, I analyze stateless mitigations, which do not use a tracker array, separately.

It is worth noting that refreshing itself, if not carefully controlled, can also be exploited by attackers to induce bit-flips. Since refreshing is essentially reading and re-writing to a row, excessive refresh operations also lead to excessive activations, so nearby rows that are refreshed act as a secondary source of hammering\cite{Kogler22}. A refresh-centric mitigation can fail if it is not aware of this effect and failed to keep track of activations due to refreshing.

\subsubsection{Insertion Policy}\label{sec:insertion}

Insertion policy decides which rows should be monitored by the tracker array. In the case an insertion policy fails to insert an aggressor row into the tracker array before it reaches $T_{RH}$ activations, a \textit{Tracker Insertion Failure (TIF)} occurs. Systems with more \textit{hot-rows}, ones that receive significant more access, also suffer from weighty performance penalty since potentially many rows need to be constantly refreshed. 

As described earlier, many mitigations (e.g.  \cite{Kim23,Bostanci24,Lin25,Olgun24,Woo25,Taneja26}) employ a counter-based approach to keep track of the activation count of potential aggressor rows,  updated upon every activation operation. Given the limited logic space (space of SRAM, Static Random-Access Memory) in DRAM modules, only very few counters can be added without significant area overhead, while placing per-row counters in DRAM leads to significant slowdown\cite{Hajiabadi25}. The Per-Row Activation Counting (PRAC) deployed in DDR5 is an instance of such mitigation\cite{JESD79-5C}. Their insertion policy, therefore, must be designed to leverage the limited number of counters to track all potential aggressors. For instance, \cite{Kim23} uses Misra-Gries to watch the most accessed rows, and \cite{Bostanci24, Nazaraliyev25} use hashing algorithm to map a counter to a group of rows to reduce the total number of counters, and \cite{Taneja26} only tracks activation count at subarray level.

The decreasing $T_{RH}$ in new DRAM devices severely influence counter-based approaches, forcing these mitigations to issue more refresh operations, which can take up memory bandwidth and drag the system if not handled properly. Jaleel et al.\cite{Jaleel24} criticize that counter-based approaches are dependent on the access-pattern, relative access frequency of different rows in particular. As it is  possible for attackers to frequently activate dummy rows to fill up the tracker array in many-sided attack, rendering the mitigation ineffective, the access-pattern dependency enables attackers to manipulate access count of dummy rows to bypass them\cite{Frigo20}.

Probabilistic insertion policy, in comparison, randomly choose rows to add to the tracker, which eliminates access-pattern dependency. PrIDE\cite{Jaleel24} is an instance of such mitigation, which can withstand lower $T_{RH}$ as the tracker array size increases. It's worth noting that probabilistic insertion policy is not deterministic, so there is a chance that the activations on aggressor rows are omitted without mitigation.

Interestingly, \cite{Demirseren24} is a juxtaposition of both approaches, dynamically adjusting refresh probability based on counter values. While this combination indeed reduces excessive refreshes, it also forgoes the advantage of strict access-pattern independence.

\subsubsection{Eviction Policy}

Specifying which rows to remove from the tracker array, eviction policy can fail when a \textit{Tracker Retention Failure (TRF)} arises, in which the tracker evicts a aggressor row without mitigating it. Per-row counting methods (\cite{Olgun24,JESD79-5C,Demirseren24,Woo25}), fortunately, does not suffer from TRF because they never evict rows.

For example, as in the case of\cite{Kim23}, where rows with lower activation count are evicted, the eviction policy is stemmed from the relative access frequency of different rows. This again allows for bypass by manipulating the ratio of activation count of dummy rows to that of real aggressor rows.

Mitigations have taken different ways to prevent TRF. \cite{Bostanci24, Jaleel24} make their eviction policy access-pattern independent, seeking a non-deterministic approach that is not easily defeated by carefully crafted attack pattern. \cite{Nazaraliyev25} retains the activation count of rows being evicted, so they will be mitigated (\textit{i.e.} neighbor rows refreshed) upon later activation despite being evicted.

Tardiness is also an issue that can potentially arise from eviction policy in conjunction with mitigation policy, in which the aggressor rows receive enough activations over $T_{RH}$ while they are waiting to be mitigated in the tracker array. We continue this discussion further in the next section.

\subsubsection{Mitigation Policy}

Mitigation policy dictates which rows in the tracker array should be mitigated. Current mitigation policies reflect a tension between low tardiness and access-pattern independence, since it is naturally difficult to obtain the urgency of mitigation without knowing how close the rows are to the $T_{RH}$ threshold. Granted, tardiness is also worsened by the fact that DDR5 allows refresh to be postponed and performed in batch later.

\cite{Demirseren24,Jaleel24} mitigate rows regardless of their current activation count, therefore having a relatively high tardiness. As such, their mitigation policy can considerably affect the minimum $T_{RH}$ they withstand, especially as the tracker array grows, as evidenced by Jaleel's experiment\cite{Jaleel24}. For instance, with First-in-First-out mitigation policy,  \cite{Qureshi25} demonstrated that it is possible to defeat the mitigation by hammering the ``youngest" row in the queue of rows to be mitigated.

\cite{Kim23,Bostanci24,Nazaraliyev25,Lin25,Olgun24,Woo25} decide to make an informed mitigation choice, prioritizing rows that have activation count over the threshold. However, whether they incur weighty slowdown when many rows hit $T_{RH}$ simultaneously or not remains unclear. Woo et al.\cite{Woo25} and Bostanci et al.\cite{Bostanci25} further demonstrates that access-pattern dependency in mitigation policy can potentially enable covert channels and leak sensitive data. Hence, the former sought to mitigate the most accessed row at fixed interval, while the latter proposed RIAC, implemented with PRAC, which sets the counter to a random number after performing mitigation so that the channel becomes heavily noised.

\subsubsection{Stateless Mitigations}

Stateless mitigations often embrace a tracker-less approach that does not require counting number of activations of any rows, and thus do not fit into Jaleel's framework of insertion, eviction, and mitigation policy. For instance, some randomly choose rows to mitigate. Randomized stateless mitigations, in general, mostly have a low implementation overhead but also suffer from tardiness issue\cite{Qureshi24}.

The earliest example of probability-based mitigation is PARA\cite{Kim14}, which has a small chance to refresh nearby rows for every row activation. The idea is that, as aggressor rows must be frequently activated, there is a high probability they will be mitigated at some point before $t_{REFI}$, likely with fewer excess refresh than counter-based mitigations and therefore lower performance overhead. Qureshi et al. Similarly, \cite{Qureshi24,Woo24} also probabilistically mitigate randomly selected rows.

\subsection{Frequency-Centric}

Frequency-centric mitigations are based on the observation that frequent activations to aggressor rows are essential to induce bit-flips. This category of defenses detect and throttle excessive activations in potential aggressor rows. They all need to be aware of when a RowHammer attack is taking place, or who might initiate an attack in static analysis approaches. Frequency-centric mitigations take many forms, ranging from static analysis to reactively throttling memory access.

Static analysis approaches are rare, with the only example being MASCAT\cite{Irazoqui18}, a code analysis tool that detects microarchitectural attacks, including RowHammer, in binaries. MASCAT looks for a specific trait of RowHammer, \textit{Cache Bypassing}. Cache bypassing is a technique used by attackers to ensure the aggressor rows are not in the CPU cache so that the memory accesses to them are guaranteed to reach DRAM.

However, \cite{Gruss18} soon found that static analysis can be defeated by placing code in Intel SGX Enclaves, a hardware-isolated, encrypted memory region that cannot be accessed even by privileged processes. Therefore, recent frequency-centric mitigations have shifted their focus focus onto on-the-fly attack detection. For instance, Zhang et al.\cite{Zhang20} proposes to detect electromagnetic waves (ER) generated by RowHammer. However, this detection method does not give information about aggressor rows or victim rows. Therefore, this work suggested to terminate all processes upon an attack, or run each process individually to identify the attacker, which can lead to significant performance overhead or service interruption.

To overcome the lack of precision, most modern frequency-centric mitigations use counter-based measures to detect ongoing attacks with greater granularity (e.g. \cite{Bennett2021,DDR5,Yaglikci21,Qureshi26}), same as described in section \ref{sec:refresh-centric}. \cite{Bennett2021} proposed Panopticon, which laid the foundation for PRAC and Alert Back-Off (ABO) in DDR5. Panopticon is based on refresh-centric mitigation, but it resolves two issues: $1.$ per-row counting occupies too much SRAM space, $2.$ memory controllers sometimes do not have time to perform all refresh for mitigations along with regular refresh.   Panopticon instead builds counters in DRAM rows and leverages the $ALERT_{n}$ signal in DDR4, originally intended for handling errors, to halt the memory controller, so mitigations can be performed. Building on Panopticon, Alert Back-Off (ABO)\cite{DDR5} has become a built-in feature of DDR5. When certain conditions are met, such as a suspected RowHammer attack is detected, the $ALERT_{n}$ signal will be set to high. The memory controller, upon detecting the $ALERT_{n}$ signal, allows for normal operation for a short time (180ns), then it must halt all operations and issue Refresh Management (RFM) command which performs preventive refreshes.

Other refresh-centric mitigations differ in their approaches to track aggressor rows and how they perform mitigation. BreakHammer\cite{Canpolat24} builds on existing refresh-centric mitigations, namely PRAC, to detect ongoing attack. BreakHammer then limits the memory bandwidth of threads that are observed to trigger excessive RowHammer-preventive actions. BlockHammer\cite{Yaglikci21} uses Bloom Filters instead of per-row counters to track activation count to reduce area overhead. BlockHammer has two layers of throttling RowHammer: BlockHammer blacklists rows that are being activated frequently, forcing a time delay on consecutive activations to blacklisted rows; it also limits the memory bandwidth of threads that are repeatedly activating blacklisted rows to free up memory for benign applications. \cite{Qureshi26} proposed SALT, which uses per-subarray counting instead of per-row counting to reduce area and performance overhead. It triggers ABO as mitigation when a subarray receives excessive activations.

Unlike refresh-centric mitigations which issues refresh that consumes memory bandwidth, frequency-centric mitigations that throttles the aggressor adds less latency: the memory bandwidth is freed up for other benign applications. ABO-like defenses that halt the entire DRAM forgoes this advantage but they can be implemented without knowledge of software-level aggressors, making them easier to implement. In both approaches, false positives can still lead to weighty slowdown, even causing Denial of Service (DoS)\cite{Nazaraliyev25}.

Frequency-centric mitigations are all based on an assumption --- frequent activation to aggressor rows is an prerequisite to induce a bit-flip. This assumption, however, does not always hold. Luo et al. has demonstrated that by keeping a DRAM row open (activated) for an extended period of time, it is possible to induce bit-flips at extremely low activation count, even as low as 1 in extreme cases\cite{Luo23}. Therefore, it is important for modern mitigations to take row activation time into account.

\subsection{Isolation-Centric}

Unlike refresh-centric mitigations, isolation-centric mitigations do not reactively mitigate rows, but instead break the spatial proximity between aggressor rows and their potential victim rows, eliminating chance of bit-flips.

Most isolation-centric mitigations are centered around two strategies: \textit{Remapping} and \textit{Partitioning} rows. Partitioning establishes physical boundaries to prevent rows with sensitive data from being affected by aggressor rows. Remapping, alternatively, intervenes in the translation from logical address to physical address, either statically or dynamically, to isolate faults or prevent disturbance from accumulating before a bit-flip is induced. The implementation level ranges from OS kernel to DRAM hardware, featuring distinct performance tradeoffs. In this section, we analyze these two approaches and their challenges.

\subsubsection{Partitioning}

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{isolation_colored.png}
	\caption{Isolation-centric mitigations based on partitioning. RowHammer cannot occur cross-subarray. Guard rows can be placed between aggressor rows and victim rows protect victims. There might be more than one guard rows in practice taking the blast radius into account.}\label{fig:isolation}
\end{figure}

Physical boundaries generally take two forms: \textit{guard rows}, and \textit{subarray isolation}, as illustrated by Fig. \ref{fig:isolation}. Guard rows are unused rows placed between potential aggressor and victim rows to "absorb" bit-flips. Due to the spatial locality of RowHammer effect, it is a lot harder for the aggressor row to induce bit-flips across all guard rows in between. The earliest partitioning approach, though not being a direct mitigation to RowHammer itself, was a bank-aware memory allocator proposed by \cite{Yun14}, which enables memory pages to be allocated to specific banks. Then, \cite{Brasser16} was the first to use guard rows to defend against RowHammer by placing one guard row between kernel space and user space, and between user space processes memory. \cite{Izzo18} soon realized that one guard row was not sufficient to defeat increasingly sophisticated attack pattern, raising the number of guard rows to 8. Later, Konoth et al.\cite{Konoth18} proposed ZebRAM, placing one guard row between every two adjacent memory rows, thereby protecting the entire DRAM. While they claimed to use guard rows for integrity check and swap space, this essentially halved the memory space available for storing conventional data. Similarly, \cite{Tatar18,Bock19} also use guard rows to protect confidential data.

This trend presents a main controversy with partitioning: \textit{capacity loss}, memory space that is rendered unusable for guarding purpose. Meanwhile, \cite{Loughlin21} criticizes that these mitigations forgo \textit{Bank-Level Parallelism (BLP)}, an important performance optimization in modern DRAM. Since memory operations on different DRAM banks can be carried out concurrently, putting memory pages from a trust domain, such as a process, in a single bank risks creating a hot-bank and serializing all subsequent memory access to this domain.

Consequently, \cite{Loughlin21} stressed the importance of utilizing subarray isolation to restore BLP. That is, subarrays within the same bank are isolated from each other as they do not share bitlines, so hammering a row in a subarray will not affect an adjacent subarray (See Fig. \ref{fig:isolation}). Therefore, memory pages from a domain can still be placed in different banks while being isolated, as long as they are in not in the same subarray. Siloz\cite{Loughlin23} is a mitigation employing this strategy: it focuses on mitigating inter-VM RowHammer attacks by placing memory lines from different VMs in different subarrays, preventing RowHammer across trust domains (VMs). Unfortunately, it is criticized by \cite{Saxena24_2} for introducing \textit{stranding}, the second challenge of partitioning, which refers to DRAM space being underutilized, as space in a subarray cannot be used by other trust domains and might remain unused by the owner. \cite{Saxena24_2,M25} sought to further reduce stranding only strictly enforce isolation on confidential data.

Apart from using guard rows and subarray-isolation, there are other approaches to break spatial correlation. NoHammer\cite{Lee23} stores the memory lines of aggressors in an extended cache area, ensuring their memory lines are never evicted from the cache. Since a memory access cannot reach DRAM if the line it accesses is already in the cache, NoHammer prevents activation to aggressor rows in the DRAM, practically moving them into the cache.

The evolution of partitioning mitigation approaches, in my observation, reflects two fundamental design tradeoff. The tradeoff between domain-aware protection and universal protection, and between capacity loss and stranding. \cite{Yun14,Brasser16,Izzo18,Tatar18,Bock19,Loughlin23,Saxena24_2,M25} have only used guard rows to protect confidential data and isolate memory space from different trust domains, as defined by the memory allocator on the OS level, which creates a semantic gap: the underlying hardware has no knowledge to decide which rows are to be protected. Conversely, universal protections like ZebRAM\cite{Konoth18} do not rely on the knowledge of trust domains, but they give up the flexibility to selectively protect rows, which leads us to the next tradeoff. Capacity loss stems from using guard rows as scapegoats, in turns losing the ability to utilize part of the memory space, whereas stranding is a result of using physical boundaries in DRAM, like banks and subarrays. Nonetheless, granting a certain domain exclusive access to a memory region prevents using the area not occupied by it currently. It also raises scalability issue because the number of trust domains in scaled system can quickly exceeds the number of subarrays.

\subsubsection{Remapping}

Remapping-based mitigations break spatial proximity by changing the neighbor rows of potential aggressor rows, so that the attacker cannot reliably identify and target a sensitive victim row. They are either static or dynamic, depending on whether the remapping is determined upon initialization or adjusted during runtime. The early example of dynamic remapping includes \textit{Randomized Row-Swap (RRS)}\cite{Saileshwar22}, in which aggressor rows are identified using counters and swapped with another randomly selected row. \cite{Saxena22,Saxena24,Wi26} also proposed mitigations alike. Dynamic remapping has a common prerequisite of identifying aggressor rows, which is usually implemented with a refresh-centric mitigation. Therefore, Rubix\cite{Saxena24} acts as an enhancement to refresh-centric mitigation approaches, aiming to reduce the number of hot-rows.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\columnwidth]{supercell_colored.png}
	\caption{A controller row is aggregated from cells with the same address in all DRAMs in the rank.}\label{fig:supercell}
\end{figure}

Dynamically shuffling rows can be expensive: it requires storing mapping relationships in precious SRAM space and incurs performance overhead for swapping rows. Static defenses, pioneered by RAMPART\cite{Woo24}, sought to address this issue. Woo et al. made the key observation that when a controller row is hammered, the corresponding address in all DRAM chips are affected because a controller row is aggregated from cells with the same address in each DRAM chip in the same bank (As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:supercell}). Accordingly, shuffling rows in each DRAM leads to errors being spread out in different logical rows. Noting that if the bit-flips are scattered in different rows, it is possible to restore the corrupted data by using \textit{Error-Correction Code (ECC)} such as \textit{Single Device Data Correction (SDDC)}, a mechanism in memory to correct data corruption. The combination of static remapping and ECC confront the attacker to defeat both of them simultaneously to produce bit-flips.

Remapping and partitioning mitigate RowHammer at the cost of spatial capacity and latency, respectively. The remapping-based mitigations are generally more transparent, eliminating the semantic gap between OS and hardware and providing universal protection that most partitioning mitigations forfeit for the sake of resource utilization, while partitioning offers a physical boundary that is easier to verify.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Limitations of This Study}

This study surveys RowHammer mitigations proposed after 2023, so some older mitigations might be underrepresented.  Though this study aims to be comprehensive, it is not necessarily exhaustive or systematic. In this study, the taxonomy proposed in Loughlin et al.'s 2021 paper\cite{Loughlin21} is employed to classify RowHammer mitigations. However, some defenses fit in multiple categories, rendering the taxonomy not mutually exclusive. For example, Alert Back-Off (ABO) is based on PRAC, a refresh-centric mitigation, but it also fits in the category of frequency-centric mitigation. To emphasis the tradeoffs in design choices, this study described many mitigations in a simplified manner, omitting some details of implementation.

\subsection{Scalability and Limitations of Current Mitigations}

In section \ref{sec:bg-rowhammer} I identified that RowHammer is worsening as the RowHammer threshold continues to decrease in latest DRAM. This poses challenges to all reactive mitigations, including refresh-centric, frequency-centric, and dynamic shuffling isolation-centric mitigation approaches, as they have to perform mitigation more frequently due to bit-flips occurring more easily. The only exception are static DRAM row remapping and domain-aware isolation, but they might also suffer from more errors that can overwhelm ECC and require additional guard rows which leads to capacity loss. Therefore, most mitigations, including the most prevalent PRAC and ABO built in DDR5, are susceptible to lower $T_{RH}$ and unscalable.
