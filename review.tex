\begin{abstract}
	RowHammer is a critical DRAM vulnerability that enables bit-flips via rapid row activations and compromises data integrity. As the storage density of DRAM scales, the RowHammer effect worsens, rendering existing mitigations increasingly costly and ineffective. This study surveys and systematizes major RowHammer mitigations, particularly those published after 2023, using the refresh-centric, frequency-centric, and isolation-centric taxonomy of Loughlin et al.
	
	By extending the review by Naseredini with recent work, this study reveals that the majority of mitigations are fundamentally unscalable against the decreasing RowHammer threshold. Beyond performance overhead, a critical yet underappreciated issue is identified: the timing side-channel inherent in access-pattern dependent defenses. Ultimately, the analysis highlights a conflict between robust security and operational efficiency, showing that existing mitigations cannot concurrently optimize for security determinism, storage density, and low-latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{S}{erving} as the essential bridge between high-speed processor caches and non-volatile storage, Dynamic Random-Access Memory (DRAM) is a ubiquitous primary storage medium that provides high-capacity, low-latency workspace needed by almost all modern workloads. The RowHammer attack\cite{Kim14} is a disturbance error that remains a significant threat to DRAM's security and reliability. It allows attackers to flip the bits of adjacent DRAM rows by frequently reading from a row. RowHammer structurally undermines memory isolation, a foundational security guarantee on which modern operating systems are built. Consequently, this enables malicious applications to compromise the availability, reliability, and confidentiality of computer systems. For example, \cite{Seaborn15} demonstrated that it is possible to escalate privilege by ``hammering" sensitive data entries in memory. The scaling of DRAM has exacerbated RowHammer: the RowHammer Threshold, the number of activations required to induce bit-flips, falls as the storage density of DRAM continues to increase, as illustrated by Fig. \ref{fig:threshold}. Many mitigations thought to be RowHammer-free were then defeated at low RowHammer thresholds\cite{Orosa21}.

Several surveys have synthesized the RowHammer mitigation literature. However, existing surveys primarily classify mitigations by implementation level (e.g., Hardware-based versus Software-based). This study adopts a different analytical lens, focusing on the mechanisms and failure modes of mitigations. It builds on Loughlin's taxonomy\cite{Loughlin21}, dividing mitigations into \textit{Refresh-Centric, Frequency-Centric}, and \textit{Isolation-Centric}.

This study leverages and critiques Loughlin's taxonomy and analyzes each group of mitigations in detail. Specifically, this study adopts Jaleel's\cite{Jaleel24} framework to assess trackers used in refresh-centric mitigations, which are data structures responsible for monitoring potential aggressor rows. This framework sees in-DRAM trackers as governed by three policies --- insertion  (determining which rows enter the tracker), eviction (managing entry replacement), and mitigation (deciding rows to preventively mitigate).

The cross-category analysis leads to the conclusion of a trilemma: all current mitigations fail to achieve deterministic-security, space-efficiency, and low latency simultaneously.

In this study, sources were identified through searches of Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, and ACM Digital Library using the terms \textit{RowHammer}, \textit{DRAM Disturbance Error}, and \textit{Mitigation}. Articles published in 2023 and onward are prioritized. As for prior works, this study refers to Naseredini's comprehensive review of RowHammer and its mitigations as a foundational reference\cite{Naseredini23}.

\section{Background}\label{sec:bg}

\subsection{DRAM Organization}
\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{drambanks_colored.png}
	\caption{High-level DRAM structure}\label{fig:dramstruct}
\end{figure}

Dynamic Random-Access Memory (DRAM) is the most common storage medium used by processors because of its high storage density.
Fig.\ref{fig:dramstruct} illustrates the overall structure of a modern DRAM module. A DRAM module consists of multiple DRAM chips, each featuring several DRAM banks that can be accessed concurrently. Within each bank, there are isolated subarrays of densely packed DRAM cells, in which a row of DRAM cells is connected through a \textit{wordline}, and a \textit{bitline} joins all cells in a column. A DRAM cell consists of an access transistor and a capacitor, and data is stored as electric charge in the capacitor. To access data, the memory controller translates a system address into a DRAM row address and issues an \textit{activate} command. This enables the corresponding wordline, transferring the charge from the capacitors of the entire row into the \textit{row buffer}. Subsequent operations are performed on the row buffer before the data is eventually restored to the original cells.

Capacitors inherently leak charge over time. Therefore, the memory controller periodically performs \textit{refresh} operations to retain data, which will read and rewrite all data in the DRAM so the capacitors are again fully charged. Specifically, the memory controller issues a refresh instruction (REF) every $t_{REFI}$, and any row is guaranteed to be refreshed within $t_{REFW}$ (Refresh Window).

\subsection{RowHammer}\label{sec:bg-rowhammer}

RowHammer is a DRAM security vulnerability wherein frequent, repeated activations of a specific wordline induce electromagnetic interference, causing predictable bit-flips in physically adjacent victim rows. In 2014, Kim et al.\cite{Kim14} first observed that, by frequently toggling wordlines, nearby cells leak their charge at an accelerated rate so that the cells cannot retain their charge before $t_{REFW}$, leading to bits-flips. A previous study\cite{Walker21} attributes circuit-level disturbance error primarily to two electromagnetic interference phenomena:  \textit{Electron Injection and Capture}, and \textit{Capacitive Crosstalk}. \cite{Cojocar20} also demonstrated that the RowHammer effect exhibits a spatial locality, meaning that the number of bit-flips in a row is negatively correlated to its distance from the \textit{aggressor row} (\textit{i.e.}, the row that is being "hammered"), which leads to the term \textit{blast radius}, the maximum distance an aggressor row can affect.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{rowhammer_thres.png}
	\caption{The RowHammer Threshold $T_{RH}$ across different generations of DRAM (Data from \cite{Saxena24,Gloor25}). Overall, $T_{RH}$ continues to decrease, and the rebound in DDR5 is attributed to built-in RowHammer mitigation in DDR5 chips\cite{Gloor25}. DDR5 would have a $T_{RH}$ lower than 10k without it.}\label{fig:threshold}
\end{figure}

To launch a RowHammer attack, the aggressor rows must be activated enough times before victim rows are refreshed (\textit{i.e.}, within $t_{REFI}$) such that the accumulated disturbance induces bit-flips. As mentioned earlier, refreshing would fully charge all capacitors in a row, which offsets the attacker's effort. Therefore, refreshing victim rows before they experience bit-flips can be used as a defense to RowHammer (further discussed in \ref{sec:refresh-centric}).

Unfortunately, the RowHammer vulnerability is worsening over time. As the storage density of DRAM continues to grow, DRAM cell size and cell-to-cell spacing shrink, resulting in the number of activations required to induce a bit-flip, denoted as $T_{RH}$ (the RowHammer Threshold), decreasing continuously over the last decade, as illustrated by Fig.\ref{fig:threshold}. In today's most prevalent DRAM devices, $T_{RH}$ is as low as 10k to 20k. This trend poses severe risks to new DRAM devices because it is easier to induce bit-flips with fewer activation counts, and an aggressor row can affect more potential victims.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{sides.png}
	\caption{(a) Single-sided RowHammer: only one row is hammered, and the rows within its blast radius are affected. (b) Double-sided RowHammer: two aggressor rows sandwich a victim row in between. (c) Many-sided RowHammer: many rows hammered simultaneously.}\label{fig:sided}
\end{figure}

RowHammer poses a severe security risk to computer systems, as it disrupts \textit{memory isolation}, where accessing a row alters data stored in nearby rows. For instance, \cite{Seaborn15} illustrated that by inducing bit-flips in \textit{Page Table Entry}, a data structure used by the OS to translate virtual memory address to physical memory address, an attacker can gain read and write permission to an arbitrary location in the memory and escalate privilege. Other works have shown that the RowHammer effect is capable of leaking confidential data, such as Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) weights\cite{Rakin22}, digital signature key\cite{Mus23}, and other processes' memory\cite{Kwong20}. Although no in-the-wild attacks have been reported, a recent survey by Heckel et al.\cite{Heckel26} shows that $12.5\%$ of the tested DRAM are vulnerable to fully automated RowHammer attacks, confirming that RowHammer remains a practical and scalable attack vector. 

Over the last decade, many mitigations have been proposed and defeated. Some sophisticated variants manipulate the \textit{access pattern}, defined as the sequence, frequency, and timing of memory access, to bypass mitigations. For instance, while the original RowHammer attack only hammers one row, subsequent double-sided attacks\cite{Seaborn15} and many-sided attacks\cite{Frigo20} leverage complex spatial patterns to amplify disturbance or overwhelm trackers. This will be discussed more in-depth in Section \ref{sec:insertion}.

\subsection{Previous Works}\label{sec:prev-work}

RowHammer has been heavily researched since its first appearance, and many mitigation strategies have been proposed accordingly. Many works have attempted to synthesize the knowledge in the field (e.g., \cite{Naseredini23,Loughlin21,Zhang24,Kim25,MutluKim20,Mutlu23,Mutlu23_2}), and some have tried to establish a uniform taxonomy for classifying RowHammer mitigations to provide insight for future research. A popular one is based on implementation level, dividing mitigations into either software-based or hardware-based. However, these two categories are too broad, and some recent works (e.g.\cite{Ojha25}) have employed a software-hardware co-design approach, blurring the line between these two categories.

The present work instead adopts the taxonomy proposed by Loughlin et al.\cite{Loughlin21}: \textit{isolation-centric, frequency-centric,} and \textit{refresh-centric}. This framework presents three categories that align with the physical conditions that can lead to disturbance errors: spatial proximity of aggressor and victim rows, frequent activation of aggressor rows, and victim rows not being refreshed before flipping, respectively. By focusing on functional mechanisms, it sheds light on why many mitigation strategies share similar \textit{failure modes}, the way in which a defense fails, and on their performance tradeoffs.

Building on the survey by Naseredini\cite{Naseredini23}, which covers mitigations up to 2023, this work classifies and evaluates major RowHammer mitigations with an emphasis on the analysis to works published from 2023 onward. By breaking down each category of mitigations to analyze their failure modes and tradeoffs, it provides insights into building fundamentally robust and efficient mitigations for future studies. 

\section{RowHammer Mitigations}\label{sec:mitigation}

To systematically categorize mitigation approaches, the primary defense mechanism in each work is evaluated. The classification is completed by assessing the core operational logic of each mitigation.

Mitigations are classified as \textit{refresh-centric} if the primary defense mechanism involves issuing refresh commands to proactively protect potential victim rows; \textit{frequency-centric} mitigations must throttle the memory operations stream; \textit{isolation-centric} mitigations must involve altering the physical locations of aggressor and victim rows.

In this section, trackers are first introduced as a universal primitive. They are data structures used to keep track of the activation counts of potential aggressor rows, which are utlized by all three groups of mitigations. Then, refresh-centric, frequency-centric, and isolation-centric mitigations are discussed one by one.

\subsection{Trackers: A Universal Primitive}\label{sec:tracker}

As described above, trackers are data structures used to track potential aggressor rows. They can be modeled as an array: a tracker stores activation counts of a set of DRAM rows and their activation counts. Generally, there are two types of trackers, depending on their locations: SRAM trackers and DRAM trackers. 

SRAM (Static Random-Access Memory) trackers are located in on-chip logic space, offering high-speed storage. However, the on-chip logic space is extremely limited, often being only a few bytes\cite{Qureshi24} . Therefore, SRAM trackers can only keep track of a few rows\cite{Hajiabadi25}, necessitating dynamical insertion and replacement of row entries. 

In contrast, DRAM trackers feature counters integrated in DRAM chips, co-located with cell arrays. Each DRAM row is given its own counter, and the chip's existing address decoding logic is reused to access the counters. First proposed by\cite{Bennett2021}, DRAM trackers have seen increased adoption because they will not lose track of any aggressor rows due to limited space. DDR5 features built-in Per-Row Activation Counting (PRAC), making it a primitive for later defenses\cite{JESD79-5C}.

In this section, trackers are analyzed based on their insertion, eviction, and mitigation policies --- an abstraction proposed by Jaleel et al.\cite{Jaleel24} for modeling tracker behavior. These three policies specify which rows to add to the tracker array, which rows to remove from the tracker, and which rows to perform mitigation on, respectively. There are three corresponding failure modes: \textit{Tracker Insertion Failure (TIF)}, \textit{Tracker Retention Failure (TRF)}, and \textit{Tardiness}. Table \ref{tab:refreshcomp} provides an overview of trackers used by some mitigations.

\subsubsection{Insertion Policy}\label{sec:insertion}

Insertion policy decides which rows should be monitored by the tracker array. As illustrated by Fig. \ref{fig:refreshpolicies}(a), a row needs to be inserted if it is suspected to be an aggressor. For instance, a row receiving excessive activations recently should probably be added to the tracker. In the case that an insertion policy fails to insert an aggressor row into the tracker array before it reaches $T_{RH}$ activations, a \textit{Tracker Insertion Failure (TIF)} occurs. DRAM trackers do not have an insertion policy, so they never experience TIF, as every row has its own counter.

SRAM trackers need to efficiently leverage a limited number of counters. In response, \cite{Kim23} uses Misra-Gries to watch only the most accessed rows, while \cite{Bostanci24, Nazaraliyev25} use a hashing algorithm to map a counter to a group of rows to reduce the total number of counters, and \cite{Taneja26} only tracks activation count at subarray level. These measures effectively keep track of every activation to identify the most accessed rows.

Many insertion policies are deterministic, which means rows with high activation count are always prioritized for insertion. In contrast, probabilistic insertion policy randomly chooses rows to add to the tracker. PrIDE\cite{Jaleel24} is an instance of such mitigation. The idea of probabilistic insertion policy is that aggressor rows are highly likely to be inserted since they occur more often in the memory access sequence. However, probabilistic mitigations are non-deterministic, so there is a chance that the activations on aggressor rows are omitted.

The key advantage of probabilistic insertion policy is access-pattern independence. Access-pattern dependency make mitigations vulnerable to carefully crafted attacks such as TRRespass\cite{Frigo20}, which overflows the tracker by hammering many rows concurrently, forcing a TIF to occur. TRRespass allows attackers to cover the real aggressor rows with dummy rows. With a probabilistic insertion policy, there is no way for an attacker to hide an aggressor row by hammering many others; every activation has an equal, independent probability of being sampled regardless of the target address.

\subsubsection{Eviction Policy}

Due to the limited space available, it is not always possible to keep every potential aggressor row in the tracker. Therefore, eviction policy specifies which rows to remove from the tracker array when the tracker is full to make room for new entries. Fig. \ref{fig:refreshpolicies}(b) demonstrates this situation, showing a possible eviction solution: evicting the row with least activations, since it is less likely to be a real aggressor row. Eviction policy can fail when a \textit{Tracker Retention Failure (TRF)} occurs, in which the tracker evicts an aggressor row without mitigating it. Per-row counting methods (\cite{Olgun24,JESD79-5C,Demirseren24,Woo25}), fortunately, do not suffer from TRF because they never evict rows.

An attacker can induce TRF by manipulating the access pattern, such as filling up the tracker with dummy rows to hide the real aggressor. Therefore, mitigations have taken different ways to prevent TRF. For instance, \cite{Bostanci24, Jaleel24} make their eviction policy access-pattern independent by not considering activation counts when evicting rows.

\subsubsection{Mitigation Policy}

Mitigation policy dictates which rows in the tracker array should be mitigated, as illustrated by Fig. \ref{fig:refreshpolicies}(c). Mitigation policy failures result in tardiness, the aggressor rows receive enough activations over $T_{RH}$ while they are waiting to be mitigated in the tracker array. Tardiness can arise from a larger tracker array size; \cite{Jaleel24} shows that a larger tracker directly increases tardiness because an entry must wait behind more rows to be mitigated.

To eliminate access-pattern dependency in mitigation policy, \cite{Demirseren24,Jaleel24} mitigate rows regardless of their current activation count. For example, \cite{Jaleel24} always mitigates the oldest entry in the tracker.  \cite{Kim23,Bostanci24,Nazaraliyev25,Lin25,Olgun24,Woo25} decide to make an informed mitigation choice, prioritizing rows that have an activation count over the threshold. Access-pattern dependency is discussed more in-depth in Section \ref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{Refresh-Centric}\label{sec:refresh-centric}

Refresh-centric mitigation approaches aim to refresh potential victim rows, ones within the blast radius of aggressor rows, before they experience bit-flips. Most refresh-centric mitigations must be implemented on the hardware level, as software lacks the ability to issue refresh to specific physical rows.

In DDR5, the introduction of the Refresh Management (RFM) command, paired with PRAC, standardized preventive refresh\cite{JESD79-5C}. RFM operations allow the memory controller to issue refresh to potential victim rows. However, RFM does not refresh an individual row; RFM instead uses PRAC to refresh all potential victim rows. Therefore, mitigations that involve issuing fine-grained refresh operations to specific rows still require modification to the memory controller or DRAM.

RFM has an additional issue: it allows refresh to be postponed and performed in batch later\cite{Qureshi24}, which worsens tardiness as described in the previous section. Attackers can exploit this increased tardiness to bypass mitigation, as highlighted by\cite{Qureshi25}: they demonstrated that it is possible to defeat mitigations by hammering the ``youngest" row in the tracker, causing bit-flips before the row is mitigated. This highlights a limitation of standardized refresh primitives, as reduced control granularity can introduce inefficiencies and delay precise mitigation.

Refresh-based defenses are categorized into stateless and stateful mechanisms. While stateful mitigations infer victim rows by using a tracker to watch the activation counts, stateless mitigations embrace a tracker-less approach. The earliest and probably most representative example of probability-based mitigation is PARA\cite{Kim14}, which has a small chance to refresh nearby rows for every row activation. The idea is that, as aggressor rows must be frequently activated, there is a high probability they will be mitigated at some point before $t_{REFI}$, likely with fewer excess refresh than counter-based mitigations and therefore lower performance overhead. \cite{Qureshi24,Woo24}  probabilistically mitigate randomly selected rows in the memory access sequence. Stateless refresh-centric mitigations mostly have a low implementation cost and low area overhead but also suffer from lack of precision. 

There is a design tradeoff between stateful and stateless mitigations: stateful mitigations use a tracker array to identify potential victims adjacent to aggressor rows, thereby providing more targeted protection but incur greater space overhead and hardware complexity; stateless mitigations probabilistically refresh adjacent rows for all or randomly chosen memory operations, offering lower overhead at the cost of security guarantee. The effectiveness of stateful mitigations primarily depends on the tracker they use, since they all issue preventive refresh as the fundamental mitigation act. As discussed previously, insertion and eviction policies determine whether aggressor rows are correctly identified (TIF / TRF), while mitigation policy controls when preventive refresh is issued, directly affecting tardiness.

A key limitation of refresh-centric mitigations is their scaling behavior under decreasing $T_{RH}$. Refresh-centric mitigations can add a significant burden to the performance, since additional refresh operations take up memory bandwidth. With the decreasing RowHammer threshold, rows will hit the threshold more easily. Benign applications can also cause additional refresh as there can be ``hot rows" that contain frequently used data, so they are often being read from. For instance, to address the decreasing $T_{RH}$, PARA needs to set the refresh probability very high, incurring more refresh for every row activation, which significantly slows down the memory. \cite{Taneja25} showed that, at $T_{RH} = 2k$, PARA can incur a slowdown of $3.6\%$, and assuming a lack of fine-grained control over refresh, the slowdown can be as high as $49\%$. The performance issue will be discussed in detail in Section \ref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{Frequency-Centric}

Frequency-centric mitigations are based on the observation that frequent activations to aggressor rows are essential to induce bit-flips. While refresh-centric mitigations are victim-focused, as they try to prevent potential victims from having bits flipped, frequency-centric mitigations are aggressor-focused: they detect and throttle excessive activations to potential aggressor rows. They all need to be aware of when a RowHammer attack is taking place, or who might initiate an attack in static analysis approaches. Frequency-centric mitigations take many forms, ranging from static analysis to reactively throttling memory access.

Static analysis approaches are rare, with the only example being MASCAT\cite{Irazoqui18}, a code analysis tool that detects microarchitectural attacks, including RowHammer, in binaries. MASCAT looks for a specific trait of RowHammer, \textit{Cache Bypassing}. Cache bypassing is a technique used by attackers to ensure the aggressor rows are not in the CPU cache so that the memory accesses to them are guaranteed to reach DRAM.

However, \cite{Gruss18} soon found that static analysis can be defeated by placing code in Intel SGX Enclaves, a hardware-isolated, encrypted memory region that cannot be accessed even by privileged processes. Therefore, recent frequency-centric mitigations have shifted their focus onto on-the-fly attack detection. For instance, Zhang et al.\cite{Zhang20} proposed to detect electromagnetic waves (ER) generated by RowHammer. However, this detection method does not give information about aggressor rows or victim rows. Therefore, this work suggested terminating all processes upon an attack or testing each process individually to identify the attacker, which can lead to significant performance overhead or service interruption.

To overcome the lack of precision, most modern frequency-centric mitigations use trackers to detect ongoing attacks with greater granularity (e.g. \cite{Bennett2021,DDR5,Yaglikci21,Qureshi26}), as described in Section \ref{sec:tracker}. \cite{Bennett2021} proposed Panopticon, which laid the foundation for PRAC and Alert Back-Off (ABO) in DDR5. Panopticon is based on refresh-centric mitigation, but it resolves an issue: memory controllers sometimes do not have time to perform all refresh for mitigations along with regular refresh, as spending too much time on refreshing can violate the deterministic timing constraints of DDR5\cite{Qureshi25}. Panopticon instead uses PRAC for attack detection and leverages the $ALERT_{n}$ signal in DDR4, originally intended for handling errors, to halt the memory controller, so mitigations can be performed. Building on Panopticon, Alert Back-Off (ABO)\cite{DDR5} has become a built-in feature of DDR5. When certain conditions are met, such as when a suspected RowHammer attack is detected, the $ALERT_{n}$ signal will be set to high. The memory controller, upon detecting the $ALERT_{n}$ signal, must halt all operations and issue an RFM command.

Other frequency-centric mitigations differ in their approaches to tracking aggressor rows and how they perform mitigation. BreakHammer\cite{Canpolat24} builds on PRAC to detect ongoing attacks and limits the memory bandwidth of threads that are observed to trigger excessive RowHammer-preventive actions. BlockHammer\cite{Yaglikci21} uses Bloom Filters instead of per-row counters to track activation count to reduce area overhead. BlockHammer has two layers of throttling RowHammer: BlockHammer blacklists frequently activated rows, imposing delays on their access and throttling threads that repeatedly target them. \cite{Qureshi26} proposed SALT, which uses per-subarray counting instead of per-row counting to reduce area and performance overhead. It triggers ABO as mitigation when a subarray receives excessive activations.

Frequency-centric mitigations impose lower latency than refresh-centric mitigations, freeing up the memory bandwidth for other benign applications. ABO-like defenses that halt the entire DRAM forgo this advantage, but they can be implemented without knowledge of software-level aggressors, making them easier to implement. In both approaches, false positives can still lead to a weighty slowdown, even causing Denial of Service (DoS)\cite{Nazaraliyev25b}.

\subsection{Isolation-Centric}

Unlike refresh-centric and frequency-centric mitigations, isolation-centric mitigations do not reactively mitigate rows, but instead break the spatial proximity between aggressor rows and their potential victim rows, eliminating the chance of bit-flips.

Most isolation-centric mitigations are centered around two strategies: \textit{Remapping} and \textit{partitioning} rows. Partitioning establishes physical boundaries to prevent rows with sensitive data from being affected by aggressor rows. Remapping, alternatively, intervenes in the translation from logical address to physical address, either statically or dynamically, to isolate faults or prevent disturbance from accumulating before a bit-flip is induced. The implementation level ranges from the OS kernel to DRAM hardware, featuring distinct performance tradeoffs. In this section, we analyze these two approaches and their challenges.

\subsubsection{Partitioning}

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{isolation_colored.png}
	\caption{Isolation-centric mitigations based on partitioning. RowHammer cannot occur cross-subarray. Guard rows can be placed between aggressor rows and victim rows to protect victims. There might be more than one guard row in practice taking the blast radius into account.}\label{fig:isolation}
\end{figure}

Physical boundaries generally take two forms: \textit{guard rows} and \textit{subarray isolation}, as illustrated by Fig. \ref{fig:isolation}. Guard rows are unused rows placed between potential aggressor and victim rows to "absorb" bit-flips. Due to the spatial locality of the RowHammer effect, it is a lot harder for the aggressor row to induce bit-flips across all guard rows in between. \cite{Brasser16} was the first to use guard rows to defend against RowHammer by placing one guard row between kernel space and user space, and between memory regions of any two processes. \cite{Izzo18} soon realized that one guard row was not sufficient to defeat increasingly sophisticated attack patterns, raising the number of guard rows to 8. Later, Konoth et al.\cite{Konoth18} proposed ZebRAM, placing one guard row between every two adjacent memory rows, thereby protecting the entire DRAM.

This trend presents a main controversy with partitioning:
capacity loss, memory space rendered unusable for
guarding purpose. Therefore, another approach is to use a bank-aware memory allocator\cite{Yun14} to place memory pages of different trust domains in different banks, so they cannot interfere with others, as banks are physically isolated from each other. Nevertheless, \cite{Loughlin21} criticizes that this approach forgoes Bank-Level Parallelism (BLP), an important
performance optimization in modern DRAM that enables simultaneous access to multiple banks. By putting memory pages from a trust domain, such as a process, in a single bank, this mitigation risks creating a ``hot-bank" and serializing all subsequent memory access to this domain.

Consequently, \cite{Loughlin21} stressed the importance of utilizing subarray isolation to restore BLP. That is, subarrays within the same bank are isolated from each other as they do not share bitlines, so hammering a row in a subarray will not affect an adjacent subarray (See Fig. \ref{fig:isolation}). Therefore, memory pages from a domain can still be placed in different banks while being isolated, as long as they are not in the same subarray. Siloz\cite{Loughlin23} is a mitigation employing this strategy: it focuses on mitigating inter-VM RowHammer attacks by placing memory lines from different VMs in different subarrays, preventing RowHammer across trust domains (VMs).

Unfortunately, it is criticized by \cite{Saxena24_2} for introducing \textit{stranding}, which refers to DRAM space being underutilized, as space in a subarray cannot be used by other trust domains and might remain unused by the owner. \cite{Saxena24_2,M25} sought to further reduce stranding by only strictly enforcing isolation on confidential data.

Apart from using guard rows and subarray-isolation, there are other approaches to break spatial correlation. NoHammer\cite{Lee23} stores the memory lines of aggressors in an extended cache area, ensuring their memory lines are never evicted from the cache. Since a memory access cannot reach DRAM if the line it accesses is already in the cache, NoHammer prevents activation of aggressor rows in the DRAM, practically moving them into the cache.

\subsubsection{Remapping}

Remapping-based mitigations break spatial proximity by changing the neighbor rows of potential aggressor rows, so that the attacker cannot reliably identify and target a sensitive victim row. They are either static or dynamic, depending on whether the remapping is determined upon initialization or adjusted during runtime. The early example of dynamic remapping includes \textit{Randomized Row-Swap (RRS)}\cite{Saileshwar22}, in which aggressor rows are identified using counters and swapped with another randomly selected row. \cite{Saxena22,Saxena24,Wi26} also proposed similar mitigations. Rubix\cite{Saxena24} acts as an enhancement to refresh-centric mitigation approaches, aiming to reduce the number of hot-rows by dynamically changing row mapping.

Dynamically shuffling rows can be expensive: it requires storing mapping relationships in precious SRAM space and incurs performance overhead for swapping rows. Static defenses, pioneered by RAMPART\cite{Woo24}, sought to address this issue. RAMPART shuffles rows within individual chips so that bit-flips are scattered across separate logic rows. This allows standard ECC (Error Correction Code) to correct the corruption, creating a dual-layer defense that requires attackers to defeat both remapping and error correction.

\subsubsection{Summary of Tradeoffs}

The evolution of partitioning mitigation approaches reflects two fundamental design tradeoffs: the tradeoff between domain-aware protection and universal protection, and between capacity loss and stranding.

\cite{Brasser16,Izzo18,Tatar18,Bock19,Loughlin23,Saxena24_2,M25} have only used guard rows to protect confidential data and isolate memory space from different trust domains, as defined by the memory allocator on the OS level, which creates a semantic gap: the underlying hardware has no knowledge to decide which rows are to be protected. Conversely, universal protections like ZebRAM\cite{Konoth18} do not rely on the knowledge of trust domains, but they give up the flexibility to selectively protect rows.

Meanwhile, capacity loss stems from using guard rows as scapegoats, losing the ability to utilize part of the memory space, whereas stranding is a result of using physical boundaries in DRAM, like banks and subarrays, because granting a certain domain exclusive access to a memory region prevents using the area not occupied by it currently.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{A Fundamental Assumption Failure}

The classical threat vector of RowHammer has evolved into a broader characterization of DRAM disturbance error. Specifically, RowPress\cite{Luo23} is a disturbance error pattern in which bit-flips occur near DRAM rows that are kept open (activated) for an extended period of time. \cite{Luo23} found that this phenomenon can substantially reduce RowHammer thresholds of nearby rows. In extreme cases, $T_{RH}$ can be decreased to as low as 1. RowPress can also cause bit-flips within a short period if combined with RowHammer\cite{Luo24_2}.  Section \ref{sec:prev-work} highlighted that all reactive mitigations, including refresh-centric, frequency-centric, and dynamic shuffling isolation-centric mitigation approaches, are based on the assumption that frequent activation of aggressor rows is a prerequisite of causing bit-flips. However, the introduction of RowPress renders this assumption completely invalid. Unless explicitly taking row open time into account, reactive mitigations expose DRAM to the threat of RowPress while seemingly protecting it. 

Granted, refresh-centric and frequency-centric mitigations are still prevalent because they are optimized for common cases, where $T_{RH}$ is far greater than 1, but RowPress reveals a deep research gap: factors that are known to influence RowHammer threshold, apart from activation count, are underappreciated.  \cite{Orosa21} identified that aggressor row active time is a key property that influences $T_{RH}$ back in 2021, and RowPress leverages this effect to break reactive mitigations in 2023. Factors that are proven to have an impact on $T_{RH}$ include temperature, victim DRAM cell's physical location, physical region of DRAM, wordline voltage, etc.\cite{Orosa21,Yaglikci22}. Although existing mitigations can be modified to prevent RowPress (e.g. \cite{Luo23,Luo24,Qureshi24_2}), this only addresses the specific mechanism RowPress exploits, not the broader class of underappreciated factors, so future attack variants can exploit said factors to bypass mitigations that are regarded as secure today. Therefore, security proofs for reactive mitigations should be regarded as conditional on the assumption that activation count is the only disturbance variable, an assumption that has been proven false by RowPress and could be further weakened by other factors.

\subsection{The Scalability Crisis}

The declining $T_{RH}$ in new DRAM devices severely affects all three types of mitigations. Refresh-centric and frequency-centric mitigations are directly influenced by this trend; \cite{Vittal25} shows that PRAC + ABO in DDR5 already leads to a 10\% slowdown on average at current $T_{RH}$. Extrapolating the trend of decreasing $T_{RH}$, we can predict that current refresh-centric and frequency-centric mitigations will cause a prohibitive slowdown. Decreasing $T_{RH}$ will also overwhelm the mitigation policy of refresh-centric mitigations: unless combined with frequency-centric mitigations like ABO, only a limited number of refresh operations can be carried out in a time window to ensure deterministic timing guarantees of DDR5\cite{Qureshi25}, so not all aggressor rows can be refreshed in a timely manner. Stateless refresh-centric mitigations based on probability also fall short in keeping up with low $T_{RH}$: they have to either set mitigation probability high to ensure security, which would add high performance overhead, or set the probability very low to improve performance, which would erode security.

Refresh-centric mitigations are further challenged by decreasing $T_{RH}$, as even refreshing itself becomes exploitable by attackers. \cite{Kogler22} demonstrated that it is possible to leverage the preventive refresh operations as a secondary source of hammering, since refreshing is essentially reading and re-writing to a row, and excessive refresh operations also lead to excessive activations.

Isolation-centric mitigations that require shuffling or quarantining aggressor rows suffer from decreasing $T_{RH}$ due to similar reasons. Other isolation-centric mitigations suffer from other scalability issues: increased blast radius and rapid growth of trust domains. While RowHammer was previously thought to influence only the two immediately adjacent rows (blast radius = 1), \cite{Kogler22} demonstrated that it is possible to hammer a row from the next row over, leading to a blast radius of 2\cite{Marazzi23}. Increased blast radius requires more guard rows to be placed between memory spaces from different trust domains, leading to more space wasted. More trust domains will also lead to performance degradation: as the number of trust domains increases, more subarrays must be dedicated to them, leading to internal fragmentation, and isolating numerous domains into dedicated subarrays also limits the bank-level parallelism that can be exploited\cite{M25}.

Shrinking of $T_{RH}$ is pushing current mitigations to a point where their performance overhead causes denial-of-service (DoS), degrading availability. Though isolation-centric mitigations are prohibitively expensive as their storage overhead reduces capacity gained by scaling, static remapping or partitioning approaches are among the few that do not lead to significant additional slowdown as $T_{RH}$ falls. Hence, breaking spatial correlation instead of issuing mitigation operations might be useful for solving the scalability issue.

\subsection{Proven Insecure or Insecurely Proven: Access-Pattern Dependency}

Across all reactive defenses, a common tradeoff arises from whether to make an informed mitigation decision or maintain access-pattern independence. \cite{Frigo20} shows that carefully crafted attacks can deceive mitigations depending on access-pattern: it is  possible for attackers to frequently activate dummy rows to fill up the tracker array in many-sided attacks, so dummy rows, instead of actual aggressors, are mitigated, rendering counting-based mitigation ineffective. 

Meanwhile, any mitigations whose observable behavior varies with access-pattern can enable a timing side-channel\cite{Woo25, Bostanci25}, a ubiquitous yet underappreciated issue actively considered by only very few works.

Timing side-channels are a covert way to leak information that arises from physical properties. RowHammer mitigations can leave observable side-effects that serve as a mean of communication between processes that are not supposed to be isolated. For example, an attacker can encode a bit by choosing whether or not to hammer a row (e.g., intentionally triggering RowHammer mitigation represents a ``1", and refraining represents a ``0"). On the receiving side, the attacker can infer the bit by observing the timing differences caused by RowHammer mitigation, such as when ABO in DDR5 slows down the memory controller upon an attack. Solutions to the timing side-channel have been proposed, including a stateless refresh-centric mitigation, FR-RFM\cite{Bostanci25}, which periodically issues RFM regardless of access-pattern, and TPRAC\cite{Woo25}, which randomly issues RFM to add noise to the side-channel. However, both of them add additional mitigation operations to existing ones, leading to performance overhead, and the latter does not completely disable the side-channel, but merely makes it more costly for transmission.

Probabilistic refresh-centric mitigations are often seen as decoupled from access-pattern, hence not affected by the timing side-channel. However, their major problem is that they are non-deterministic, so there is always a chance for a successful attack. Some studies mathematically prove that their probabilistic mitigations are secure, but they might still fall due to unforeseen attack variants or incorrect underlying assumptions, as supported by \cite{Anderson23}, which further argues that security proofs can be unreliable or misleading because of factors such as abstraction errors.

Access-pattern dependency introduces a central tension: either accepting an approach that is already proven insecure, risking enabling timing side-channel attacks and being deceived by many-sided attacks, or using probabilistic mitigations that appear secure but remain non-deterministic. Therefore, this dilemma cannot be resolved by reactive mitigations, leaving proactive mitigations that focus on prevention rather than reaction as a better choice.

\subsection{Evaluation Gap}

RowHammer research will benefit from a more uniform benchmark and evaluation framework. It was found that many papers choose different benchmarks to present the performance and security guarantee of their mitigations, which prevents a direct comparison with other mitigations. A survey by Anderson and Boucher\cite{Heckel26_2} also raises questions about the general validity of some RowHammer studies, many of which use a small sample size and yield results not comparable with other studies. Therefore, building a uniform framework for mitigation analysis is urgently needed so that the field can reach a consensus on the effectiveness of mitigations.

\subsection{Limitations of This Study}

This study puts an emphasis on those proposed after 2023, so some older mitigations might be underrepresented.  Though this study aims to be comprehensive, it is not necessarily exhaustive. To emphasize the tradeoffs in design choices, this study describes many mitigations in a simplified manner, omitting some details of implementation, and did not include quantitative analysis.

\section{Conclusion}

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{unholytri.png}
	\caption{The impossible trinity: none of the current mitigations can be efficient in space and time while guaranteeing deterministic-security. Mitigations on each edge forgo the advantage indicated at the opposite node. Stateful refresh-centric and frequency-centric mitigations introduce high slowdown; isolation-centric mitigations waste DRAM space; stateless probabilistic mitigations do not guarantee deterministic security.}\label{fig:unholy}
\end{figure}

This study surveys RowHammer mitigations and classifies them into three groups: refresh-centric, frequency-centric, and isolation-centric, while further categorizing mitigations in each group to analyze their design tradeoffs. The taxonomy proposed in Loughlin et al.'s 2021 paper\cite{Loughlin21} is employed for classification. However, some defenses fit in multiple categories, rendering the taxonomy not mutually exclusive. This observation shows the necessity to take a hybrid approach to combine the strengths of multiple types of mitigations. By considering the common strengths and downsides of each group of mitigations, this study further concludes that current mitigations cannot simultaneously fulfill space-efficiency, low-latency, and deterministic-security (as illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:unholy}). Understanding the inherent tradeoffs in current defenses is essential for guiding the design of the next generation of robust and efficient memory protection mechanisms.
