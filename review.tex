\section{Introduction}
Proposed by Kim et al.\ in 2014\cite{Kim14}, RowHammer has raised significant attention in the academia and industry. The RowHammer attack targets modern DRAM with high cell density due to the scaling of DRAM process technology, which are inherently more prone to disturbance error, a fault caused by electromagnetic interference between adjacent DRAM cells. Kim et al. shows that, by frequently reading from the same address in DRAM, it is possible to induce bit-flips in nearby address, thereby corrupting data. 

Many works have demonstrated the far-reaching impact of RowHammer, ranging from gaining kernel privilege (e.g.\cite{Seaborn15}) to leaking confidential data (e.g.\cite{Rakin22,Mus23,Kwong20}). Accordingly, copious mitigations have been proposed.

In this work, we employ the taxonomy proposed by Loughlin et al.\ in 2021\cite{Loughlin21}, which contains three categories: frequency-centric; isolation-centric; refresh centric.

\section{Background}

\subsection{DRAM Organization and RowHammer}
\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{drambanks_colored.png}
	\caption{High-level DRAM structure}\label{fig:dramstruct}
\end{figure}

Fig.\ref{fig:dramstruct} illustrates the overall structure of a modern DRAM module, which consists of multiple DRAM chips, each featuring several DRAM banks that can be accessed independently. Within each bank there are arrays of densely packed DRAM cells, made up of an access transistor and a capacitor. The bit is stored as electric charge in the capacitor, and the transistor, enabled by the word line, allows the charge in capacitor to be carried to the row buffer via bitline.

To read from a DRAM row, the row has to be activated, which asserts the corresponding row line and loads the row to the row buffer. Subsequent read and write operations can be carried out on the row buffer. Due to the destructive nature of this operation, data has to be written back to the capacitor afterwards and the wordline must be set to low, known as closing the row. Additionally, capacitors inherently leak charge over time. Therefore, memory controller periodically refreshes the memory, reading and rewriting all data in the DRAM. Specifically, memory controller issues a refresh instruction (REF) every $t_{REFI}$, and any row is guaranteed to be refreshed within $t_{REFW}$ (Refresh Window).

The high density of DRAM cells makes them especially prone to electromagnetic interference. Kim et al.\cite{Kim14} discovered that by changing the voltage in a wordline repeatedly, an attacker can reliably induce bit-flips in nearby rows, later known as RowHammer.

\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{rowhammer_thres.png}
	\caption{RowHammer Threshold $T_{RH}$ across different generations of DRAM (Data from \cite{Saxena24,Gloor25}). Overall, $T_{RH}$ continues to decrease, and the rebound in DDR5 is attributed to TRR mechanisms, a RowHammer mitigation, in DDR5 chips\cite{Gloor25}}\label{fig:threshold}
\end{figure}

To launch a RowHammer attack, the aggressor rows must be activated enough times before victim rows are refreshed (\textit{i.e.} within $t_{REFI}$) such that the accumulated disturbance induces bit-flips, since refreshing would offset the attacker's effort as capacitors are re-charged. Unfortunately, RowHammer vulnerability is worsening over time. As the storage density of DRAM continues to grow, the number of activations required to induce a bit-flip, denoted as $T_{RH}$ (RowHammer Threshold), has been continuously decreasing over the last decade, as illustrated by Fig.\ref{fig:threshold}. In today's most prevalent DRAM devices, $T_{RH}$ is as low as 10k to 20k. Certain factors, like access pattern, can further lower this number. Aggressor rows also have increased \textit{blast radius}, affecting rows further away. This trend poses severe risks to new DRAM devices because they become more vulnerable to RowHammer-like attack.

\subsection{Previous Works}

RowHammer has been heavily researched since its first debut, and many mitigation strategies have been proposed accordingly. Many works have attempted to establish a uniform taxonomy for classifying RowHammer mitigations to provide insight for future research. A popular one is based on implementation level, dividing mitigations into either software-based or hardware-based. However, some recent works (e.g.\cite{Ojha25}) have employed a software-hardware co-design approach, blurring the line between these two categories.

We instead adopt the taxonomy proposed by Loughlin et al.\cite{Loughlin21}: \textit{isolation-centric, frequency-centric,} and \textit{refresh-centric}. Each category corresponds to one condition for RowHammer, including spatial proximity of aggressor and victim rows, frequent activation of aggressor rows, and victim rows not being refreshed before flipping, respectively. This taxonomy sheds light on the tradeoffs and failure modes that are common to a type of mitigation approaches. 

In this work, we comprehensively classify and evaluate major RowHammer mitigations. We build on the survey by Naseredini\cite{Naseredini23} which covers the mitigations up to 2023, and extend the analysis to works published from 2023 onward.

Refresh-centric mitigation approaches aim to refresh potential victim rows, ones within the blast radius of aggressor rows, before they experience bit-flips. Therefore, most refresh-centric mitigations must be implemented on the hardware level, as software cannot directly refresh specific address of DRAM. Generally, this category of defenses uses either counters to keep track of potential aggressor rows or probabilistically refresh rows, each featuring some design tradeoffs.

Counter-based mitigations record the activation count of \textit{hot-rows}, ones that receive significant more access, and perform mitigation. However, given the limited logic space (SRAM space) in DRAM modules, only very few counters can be added without significant area overhead. This challenges refresh-centric mitigations to make smart design choices, as using too few counters risks losing track of some aggressor rows, while it is possible for attackers to frequently activate dummy rows to fill up the tracker array\cite{Frigo20}, rendering the mitigation ineffective. Systems with more hot-rows also suffer from weighty performance penalty since potentially many rows need to be constantly refreshed. Jaleel et al.\cite{Jaleel24} criticize that counter-based approaches are dependent on the relative access frequency of different rows, which enables attackers to manipulate access count of dummy rows to bypass them.

Kim et al.\cite{Kim23} proposes an Alert Refresh System (ARS), which employs the Misra-Gries algorithm to keep track of the count of ACTIVE commands on most accessed rows, thereby identifying potential aggressor rows. ARS then identify and refresh potential victim rows that are next to the aggressor rows. Notably, their system does not require change to the memory controller or DRAM interface in contrast to many other mitigations.

Bostanci et al.\cite{Bostanci24} proposes CoMeT, a system that employs hash functions to track activation count and aggressor rows. CoMeT achieved effective prevention with relatively fewer preventive activations and lower number of counters and thus area overhead. Notably, it outperforms some then state-of-the-art mitigations at low $T_{RH}$.

Demirseren et al.\cite{Demirseren24} proposes a hybrid mitigation of Target Row Refreshing (TRR) and Probabilistic Adjacent Row Activation (PARA), using gradient ascent algorithm to dynamically adjust the refreshing probability, thereby reducing excessive activations.

Jalee et al.\cite{Jaleel24} suggests that some previous refresh-centric solutions fail because they rely on counters to decide rows to keep track of and potential victims to be refreshed, allowing attacks to bypass mitigation of aggressor rows by filling the tracker array with dummy rows due to the limited size of tracker array. Proposing PrIDE instead, they employ a probabilistic insertion policy and FIFO eviction and mitigation policy, which eliminates the access-pattern dependency.

Pointing to the limited space available for trackers, Qureshi et al.\cite{Qureshi24} also proposes a probabilistic mitigation approach, MINT, which randomly selects a row to mitigate for each REFRESH event.


\subsection{Frequency-Centric}

Criticizing that Refresh-Centric mitigations introduce significant performance overhead at low $T_{RH}$, Canpolat et al.\cite{Canpolat24} proposes BreakHammer to add another layer of prevention measure on top of existing Refresh-Centric mitigations, modifying the memory controller such that it limits the memory bandwidth of threads that are observed to trigger excessive RowHammer-preventive actions. 

\subsection{Isolation-Centric}

Saxena et al.\cite{Saxena24} proposes a optimization technique to current refresh-centric mitigations, Rubix, that also aids prevention of RowHammer on its own. Noting that memory remapping is the cause of excessive hot-rows (\textit{i.e.} DRAM rows with activation count greater than $T_{RH}$), and most refresh-centric approaches suffer from lower $T_{RH}$ due to growing number of hot-rows, Rubix breaks the physical adjacency of spatially proximate lines by introducing randomized mapping, either by encrypting the line-address (Rubix-S) or dynamic randomization (Rubix-D).
